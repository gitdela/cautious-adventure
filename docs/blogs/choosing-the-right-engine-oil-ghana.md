Stand in front of the lubricant shelf at any station shop and you face a wall
of numbers: 5W-30, 10W-40, 20W-50, API SN, SP, CI-4. The labels are written
for engineers, but the decision belongs to every driver — and in Ghana, where
engines run hot, idle long and breathe dust, it is a decision with real
consequences. The wrong oil will not stop your car tomorrow. It will quietly
shorten the life of the most expensive component you own.

This guide translates the labels, explains what our driving conditions
actually demand, and gives you a practical way to choose — whether you drive
a nearly-new saloon, a ten-year-old imported SUV, or a hard-working taxi.

## What engine oil actually does

Oil is usually described as lubrication, which undersells it. In a running
engine, oil is doing four jobs at once:

1. **Lubricating** — keeping a microscopic film between metal surfaces moving
   at thousands of RPM so they never actually touch.
2. **Cooling** — carrying heat away from parts the radiator cannot reach,
   like pistons and bearings. In slow traffic, oil does a large share of the
   cooling work.
3. **Cleaning** — holding soot, combustion by-products and microscopic wear
   metal in suspension until the filter catches them or the oil is drained.
4. **Sealing and protecting** — helping piston rings seal against the
   cylinder wall and coating parts against corrosion.

Every one of these jobs gets harder in heat, in stop-start traffic and in
dust. Which is to say: every one of these jobs is harder in Ghana than in the
mild European conditions many imported cars were specified for.

## Decoding the label

### Viscosity: the "20W-50" part

Viscosity is the oil's thickness — its resistance to flow. The two numbers
describe it at two temperatures:

- The number **before the W** ("winter") describes cold-start flow. Lower
  means thinner when cold, so oil reaches the top of the engine faster at
  start-up.
- The number **after the dash** describes thickness at full operating
  temperature. Higher means the oil stays thicker when hot.

In Ghana we can largely ignore the cold-weather anxiety the first number was
invented for — a 30 °C morning is not a Norwegian winter. What matters here
is the second number: **hot-running protection**. This is why 15W-40 and
20W-50 have long served older engines well in West Africa, while modern
engines are built with tighter internal clearances and specify thinner oils
like 5W-30 — and genuinely need them, because a thick oil cannot flow through
passages designed for a thin one.

The rule that resolves it: **the number your handbook specifies beats any
general advice**, including ours.

### Quality: the API letters

The API code (API SN, SP for petrol; CI-4, CK-4 for diesel) describes the
oil's quality generation, and the letters advance alphabetically — SP is a
newer, higher standard than SN. Newer standards are backward-compatible:
an SP oil is fine in an engine that asked for SN. The reverse is not true.
On a shelf of unfamiliar brands, the API code is your fastest quality check —
and a genuine one only if the oil itself is genuine, which is a real caveat:
counterfeit lubricants in second-hand containers are common in open markets.
Buy oil from a source that can answer for its supply chain.

### Synthetic vs mineral

Mineral oil is refined from crude; synthetic oil is engineered molecule-up to
resist heat and last longer. Semi-synthetic sits between. Synthetics hold
viscosity better at high temperature and degrade more slowly — both of which
matter more in Accra traffic than in the temperate markets the price gap was
calculated for. The practical guidance:

- **Modern engines (roughly 2010 onward), turbocharged engines** — synthetic
  or semi-synthetic, and non-negotiable for turbos, which cook oil in their
  bearings after shutdown.
- **Older, high-mileage engines** — a good mineral or semi-synthetic 15W-40 /
  20W-50 is often the better match for looser clearances, at a friendlier
  price.

## What Ghanaian conditions change

**Heat.** Ambient 30–35 °C raises the whole system's operating temperature.
Oil oxidises — degrades — roughly twice as fast for every 10 °C rise. An oil
that lasts 10,000 km in Frankfurt is living a harder, shorter life here.

**Traffic.** Idling and crawling through Kaneshie or Circle is the worst duty
an engine oil faces: the engine is hot, airflow is minimal, and the odometer
barely moves. This is why "severe service" in your handbook — with its
shorter intervals — describes ordinary city driving here, not some rare
extreme. An hour in traffic ages your oil while adding 5 km to the odometer,
which brings us to the most common maintenance mistake in Ghana:

**Changing oil by kilometres alone.** For a city-driven car, time and
engine-hours matter as much as distance. The practical rule: **follow the
severe-service interval in your handbook, and change at least twice a year
even if the kilometres have not arrived.**

**Dust.** Harmattan dust that gets past a tired air filter ends up in your
oil, where it works as a grinding paste. Your air filter is part of your oil
system: change it on schedule, and more often if you drive unpaved routes.

## A practical chooser

| Your vehicle | Sensible choice | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Modern saloon/SUV (2010+) | Synthetic or semi-synthetic 5W-30 / 5W-40 per handbook | Do not "upgrade" to thicker oil; the engine was not designed for it |
| Older or high-mileage engine | Mineral or semi-synthetic 15W-40 / 20W-50 | Rising oil consumption between changes — check the dipstick monthly |
| Turbocharged (petrol or diesel) | Full synthetic, correct spec, strictly on time | Idle 30–60 seconds before shutdown after hard driving |
| Taxi / commercial / ride-hailing | Heavy-duty oil, severe-service intervals | Track engine-hours or months, not just kilometres |

Three habits that cost nothing and catch almost everything:

1. **Check the dipstick monthly** — level and colour. Milky oil (coolant
   contamination) and gritty oil are see-a-mechanic-today findings.
2. **Never mix leftover oils** of different grades to top up. Carry a small
   bottle of the oil your engine actually uses.
3. **Change the filter with every oil change.** A litre of old oil and its
   contaminants live in a reused filter, seeding the fresh fill.

## The short version

Match the viscosity to your handbook, the quality standard to at least what
your handbook asks, and the change interval to Ghanaian conditions rather
than the optimistic sticker figure. Buy from a source that stands behind
authenticity. Do those four things and your engine's life is measured in
decades — skip them and no brand of oil, however premium, will save it.
