Ask any transport or logistics manager in Ghana what keeps them up at night
and fuel will be in the first sentence. For a typical distribution fleet, fuel
is 30–40% of total operating cost — bigger than salaries, bigger than
maintenance, bigger than financing. It is also the cost line with the most
leaks: some visible, most not.

The good news is that fuel cost is unusually controllable. Unlike cedi
depreciation or pump-price adjustments, the biggest drivers of fleet fuel
spend are inside your gate. This guide covers the five places fleets lose
money on fuel, how to measure each one, and what a structured fuelling
programme does that cash-and-receipts never will.

## First, get one number right: litres per 100 km, per vehicle

You cannot manage what you measure in cedis alone. Pump prices move; a fuel
bill that rose 8% this quarter tells you nothing about whether your fleet got
less efficient or fuel got more expensive.

The number that matters is **consumption per vehicle: litres per 100 km**
(or km per litre, if your drivers think in those terms). It is
price-independent, comparable across months, and comparable across vehicles
doing similar work. Everything else in this guide depends on having it.

To get it you need two disciplined records per vehicle:

1. **Every litre fuelled**, with date and odometer reading at fill-up.
2. **Every kilometre run**, from the same odometer.

Do this for one month and you will have a baseline. Do it for three and you
will have something better: a normal range for each vehicle. From then on,
fuel management is largely the art of noticing when a vehicle leaves its
range and asking why within days, not at year-end.

## The five leaks

### 1. Pilferage — the leak everyone suspects and few can prove

Fuel theft in fleets rarely looks like siphoning at midnight. It looks like:

- Fuelling 60 litres and recording 70, with the attendant splitting the
  difference.
- Fuelling a private car on the company account.
- "Losing" receipts and reconstructing them generously.

Cash fuelling makes all three easy, which is why the single highest-return
change most fleets can make is **taking cash out of the fuelling loop**. A
fleet account with a fuel supplier — vehicle-specific cards or an authorised
vehicle list, litres billed centrally, every transaction logged with station,
date, time and volume — turns pilferage from an accusation you cannot prove
into an anomaly that shows up in a report. When fuelling records carry the
vehicle's number plate and the odometer, a 70-litre fill on a vehicle with a
60-litre tank is not a suspicion. It is a line item.

### 2. Consumption drift — the leak nobody suspects

A vehicle that quietly went from 28 L/100 km to 32 L/100 km is burning about
14% more money every day, and no single fill-up looks wrong. Common causes,
roughly in order of frequency:

- **Tyre pressure.** Underinflated tyres on a loaded truck can add several
  percent to consumption on their own. Weekly checks are the cheapest fuel
  intervention that exists.
- **Overdue servicing.** Clogged air filters, tired injectors and old engine
  oil all show up at the pump before they show up as breakdowns.
- **Overloading.** Every tonne above rated load costs fuel and shortens the
  vehicle's life. If your consumption benchmark assumes rated load, overloads
  will surface as drift.

This is where the per-vehicle baseline earns its keep: drift is invisible in
a total fuel bill and obvious in a per-vehicle trend line.

### 3. Idling and driver habit

An idling truck engine burns roughly 2–4 litres per hour producing nothing.
Drivers idling through a two-hour loading window, air conditioning running
during long waits, aggressive acceleration between Accra traffic lights —
driver behaviour commonly separates the best and worst consumption in a fleet
running identical vehicles on identical routes by 10–15%.

The fix is not surveillance; it is feedback. Fleets that share per-driver
consumption figures monthly — and say thank you to the top of the table —
consistently close most of that gap without a single disciplinary case.

### 4. Route and scheduling waste

Kilometres you did not need to run are the purest form of fuel waste: fuel
burned at 100% of cost for 0% of revenue. The usual culprits are empty return
legs, deliveries scheduled into peak Accra or Kumasi congestion that could
have moved at 05:00, and ad-hoc dispatching that sends two half-full vehicles
where one would do. None of this needs software to find — a week of honest
route logs usually pays for the exercise many times over.

### 5. Fuel quality — the leak that arrives from outside

Fuelling wherever happens to be nearest exposes your fleet to the worst
station on your route. Off-spec or adulterated fuel raises consumption
immediately and raises maintenance cost permanently — injector and fuel-pump
damage from bad diesel is some of the most expensive routine damage a fleet
absorbs, and it is entirely avoidable. Restricting fuelling to a network with
audited quality control (we have written about [how that chain of custody
works](./how-we-guarantee-clean-fuel-in-full-quantity.md)) is not brand
loyalty; it is risk management with a maintenance line attached.

## What a structured fleet programme actually changes

Everything above can be done with a notebook and discipline. What a fleet
fuelling programme with a supplier like PETROSOL adds is that the discipline
stops depending on people remembering:

- **Cashless, vehicle-tied fuelling** across the station network — the
  pilferage controls from leak #1, enforced by the system rather than the
  supervisor.
- **Consolidated billing and per-vehicle statements** — your litres,
  kilometres and consumption trends arrive as a report instead of a
  spreadsheet someone has to maintain.
- **One negotiated commercial relationship** — predictable terms, a single
  point of accountability for quality, and bulk supply into your own tanks
  where volumes justify it.
- **Consistent fuel quality** at every fuelling point, which quietly protects
  the maintenance budget as much as the fuel budget.

## Where to start on Monday

1. Pick your five highest-mileage vehicles and start the litres + odometer
   log. Do nothing else for a month.
2. Check tyre pressures fleet-wide this week. It is the fastest payback
   available.
3. Pull three months of fuel receipts and look for fills that exceed tank
   capacity. You are not looking for a culprit; you are sizing the problem.
4. When the baseline exists, talk to a supplier about a fleet account — and
   bring the baseline, because it is also your negotiating position.

Fuel will stay your biggest cost line. It does not have to stay your leakiest
one.
