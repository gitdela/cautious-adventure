Every driver in Ghana has heard the stories. A friend whose injectors clogged
weeks after filling up at an unfamiliar station. A fleet manager whose fuel
records never quite matched the litres his trucks actually received. Fuel is
one of the few products you buy that you cannot see, smell-test or count on
delivery — you trust the nozzle, and you drive off.

That trust is the entire business we are in. "Clean fuel in full quantity" is
not a slogan we print on banners; it is a chain of specific, repeated checks
that every litre passes through before it reaches your tank. This post walks
through that chain, step by step, so you know exactly what stands behind the
promise.

## Why fuel quality is worth worrying about

Fuel adulteration — typically blending petrol or diesel with cheaper products
to stretch volumes — is a persistent problem across West African markets. The
damage it does is slow and expensive:

- **Clogged injectors and fuel pumps.** Contaminants and off-spec blends leave
  deposits that restrict fuel flow, causing rough idling, hesitation and
  eventually costly repairs.
- **Reduced engine life.** Diesel with the wrong cetane rating or petrol with
  the wrong octane rating forces the engine to work outside its design limits.
  You will not notice it in a week; you will notice it at 120,000 km instead
  of 250,000 km.
- **Worse fuel economy.** Adulterated fuel carries less usable energy per
  litre. You pay full price for fuel that takes you fewer kilometres — a
  hidden surcharge on every tank.
- **Invalidated warranties.** Vehicle manufacturers can and do reject warranty
  claims when analysis shows non-spec fuel caused the damage.

Under-dispensing is the quieter cousin of adulteration: a pump calibrated to
read 50 litres while dispensing 48 steals from you invisibly, two litres at a
time. Neither problem is detectable by the customer at the point of sale —
which is exactly why the checks have to happen before you ever arrive.

## The chain of custody: three checkpoints, every litre

### 1. At the depot: test before we load

Fuel enters our supply chain at licensed depots regulated by Ghana's National
Petroleum Authority (NPA). Before any product is loaded onto a bulk road
vehicle (BRV) for our network, it is sampled and tested against national
specifications — density, appearance, and the marker tests that reveal
dilution or product mixing. Fuel that fails does not load. There is no
"acceptable margin" here; the specification is the floor, not the target.

### 2. In transit: sealed and tracked

Adulteration most often happens between the depot and the station — a tanker
stops, product is siphoned off, and something cheaper tops the compartment
back up. We close that window two ways:

- **Compartment seals.** Every BRV compartment is sealed at the loading
  gantry. Seal numbers are recorded on the waybill, and a station is required
  to reject any delivery arriving with a broken or mismatched seal.
- **Journey monitoring.** Delivery routes and timings are tracked, so an
  unexplained stop or a delivery that took three hours longer than it should
  have is a question that gets asked — before the fuel goes into the ground.

### 3. At the station: test before we sell

When a tanker arrives at a PETROSOL station, the receiving process is not
"open the valve." Station staff verify seals, then sample each compartment and
run density and appearance checks against the depot's loading figures. Only
after the delivery passes is it discharged into the underground tanks. Tanks
themselves are dipped and monitored for water intrusion — a common quality
failure that has nothing to do with adulteration and everything to do with
poor housekeeping, which is why our maintenance schedule treats tank integrity
as seriously as pump servicing.

## Full quantity: how pump accuracy is kept honest

Quality is half the promise. The other half is that the display on the pump
tells the truth.

Fuel dispensers drift. Mechanical meters wear; a pump that was accurate at
installation can under-dispense years later without anyone intending fraud.
The defence is calibration on a schedule, not calibration when someone
complains:

- **Routine proving.** Our pumps are checked with certified proving measures —
  calibrated containers that reveal exactly how much a "10 litre" dispense
  actually delivered. Pumps outside tolerance are recalibrated or taken out of
  service.
- **Ghana Standards Authority verification.** Independent statutory
  verification puts an external check on our internal one. The seals you see
  on our dispensers are not decoration; they certify the meter was verified
  and has not been tampered with since.
- **NPA monitoring.** The National Petroleum Authority runs its own
  unannounced quality and quantity checks across the industry. We do not get
  to schedule these, which is rather the point — and our record in them is a
  commendation we are proud of.

## What you can check yourself

You should not have to verify your fuel — that is our job — but an informed
customer keeps every station in the industry honest. Three habits worth
having:

1. **Watch the pump reset to zero** before fuelling begins. Every attendant at
   our stations is trained to show you the zeroed display; if you are anywhere
   this does not happen, insist on it.
2. **Keep your receipts and track your consumption.** A sudden, sustained drop
   in kilometres-per-tank on the same route is the earliest signal of a fuel
   problem — and with receipts, a traceable one.
3. **Report anything odd.** A smell, a colour, an engine that runs differently
   after one specific fill-up. Our station managers are required to log and
   escalate quality complaints, and every complaint is traceable back through
   the delivery chain described above — that is what the seals and waybills
   are for.
